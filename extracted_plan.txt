1. How to Use This Plan
This is a five-week, full-time backend development plan. It takes an engineer from wherever they are now to being able to design, build, secure, test and ship a Spring Boot service without supervision.
Everything converges on one thing submitted at the end: a Recruitment Management Platform, delivered as a GitHub repository. The study material is sequenced so that each week teaches exactly what the next slice of that project needs. Nothing here is theory for its own sake.

1.1 The shape of each week
Every week in Section 6 has the same five parts. Work through them in order.
Part
What to do with it
Objectives
The capabilities you should have by the end of the week. Read these first — they tell you what to pay attention to in everything that follows.
Topics
The concepts to cover. Use this as a checklist; if you finish the week and one of these is still fuzzy, the week is not finished.
Watch
LinkedIn Learning courses, with the specific scope needed. Watch at 1.25× or 1.5× and code along — passive watching does almost nothing.
Read
Official documentation and book chapters. The reference docs are the primary source. Building the habit of going there before searching for a blog post is the single biggest difference between engineers who get unstuck quickly and those who do not.
Build
The part of the project this week's learning unlocks, pushed to GitHub. You do not understand a topic until you have made something with it fail and then made it work.

Each week closes with a self-check: questions to answer from memory. Being unable to answer one is a signal to go back, not a reason to move on.

A rough split of your time
Roughly 30% watching and reading, 70% building — see Section 1.2 for what that means in hours.
Front-load the watching to the start of each week so the rest of the week is hands-on.
If you find yourself watching more than building for two days running, stop and go write code.
1.2 The pace
This is an intensive plan and the days are full. Understanding that before starting is the single most important thing about what follows.
In practice it means long days, little slack between topics, and no week where you coast. A concept introduced on Monday is applied on Tuesday. There is almost no buffer to absorb a day spent stuck, which is why the 45-minute rule in Section 4.2 matters so much.

What a normal week looks like
Expect 45–50 hours, and to be genuinely tired by Friday. That is the deal, and it should be stated openly rather than discovered in Week 2.
Roughly 12–15 hours a week of watching and reading, and 30–35 hours of building. The reading is not optional and it is not homework to skip — it is the part that makes the building go faster.
Sustainable does not mean easy, but it does mean sustainable. Working past 8pm regularly, or through a weekend, is a signal that something is wrong with the plan or the support around it — raise it rather than absorbing it.

Set this up in the first two days and then leave it alone.

Branch protection on main: no direct pushes, at least one approving review on every pull request.
GitHub Issues for tracking work, with the project broken into issues before Week 2 begins.

4. Working Practices and Design Principles
These are not optional extras around the technical work. They matter as much as the code, and they are a large part of what separates an engineer who is useful in month two from one who is useful in month eight.
4.1 Definition of Done
A piece of work is not finished until every line below is true. Write this somewhere visible.
The acceptance criteria are met.
The code is formatted by Spotless and has no compiler or linter warnings.
Unit tests cover the new logic; integration tests cover the new endpoint.
The full build passes locally and in GitHub Actions.
A pull request has been opened, reviewed and approved.
OpenAPI documentation reflects any API change.
The GitHub issue is closed with a one-line summary of what was actually built.
Nothing secret is committed — no passwords, keys, tokens or real personal data.
4.2 The 45-minute rule
Struggle with a problem for 45 minutes. Then ask — in writing, including what you tried and what you observed.
Asking after 45 minutes is good judgement. Asking after four hours costs the project. Asking after four minutes costs your learning. Nobody will think less of you for asking; the only thing that looks bad is silently burning a day. With five weeks in total, a day lost is a meaningful share of everything you have.
4.3 Code review
You will both give and receive reviews on GitHub. Reviewing code is a learned skill, not an innate one, and reading someone else's solution to a problem you also solved is one of the fastest ways to improve.
Keep pull requests small. A 200-line PR gets a real review; a 2,000-line PR gets a rubber stamp.
Write a description that says what changed and why, not just what.
When reviewing, comment on design and correctness. Formatting is the tool's job.
When receiving a review, treat every comment as a question about the code rather than about you. Push back when you disagree, with reasoning.
4.4 Using AI coding assistants
You will use them. The rules are simple and there is only one that really matters.
The rule that matters: any code you submit, you must be able to explain line by line. This gets checked at every review point.
Avoid them in Week 1. The struggle in that week is where the foundations come from, and shortcutting it will cost you in Week 5.
From Week 2 onward, use them freely for boilerplate, test data, documentation drafts and explaining unfamiliar errors.
Not permitted for the starting-point check in Section 5, or in the final question-and-answer session.
When an assistant gives you a confidently wrong answer — and it will — treat that as the lesson. Learning to distrust fluent output is now a core engineering skill.
4.5 SOLID
Five principles that describe what maintainable object-oriented code looks like. They are easy to recite and hard to apply, and the gap between those two things is most of what this section is about.
Do not treat them as rules to obey mechanically. Every one of them can be over-applied into an unreadable mess of tiny interfaces. Treat them as names for problems you will feel in the code — when a class is painful to change, one of these is usually why.

Principle
What it says
What it looks like in this project
Single Responsibility
A class should have one reason to change.
Your CandidateService handles candidate logic. It does not also parse PDFs, send email and build JWTs. When you catch yourself naming something Manager or Helper, you have usually found a violation.
Open/Closed
Open for extension, closed for modification.
Adding a new CV format should mean adding a new parser class, not adding another branch to an if/else chain inside the existing one.
Liskov Substitution
A subtype must be usable anywhere its parent is, without surprises.
Any AuthenticationProvider you write must behave like an AuthenticationProvider — same contract, same exception types. If callers need to know which implementation they have, the abstraction is wrong.
Interface Segregation
Many small interfaces beat one large one.
A narrow CvParser interface with one method is easier to implement and test than a broad CandidateOperations interface whose implementers throw UnsupportedOperationException for half of it.
Dependency Inversion
Depend on abstractions, not concretions.
This is the one Spring is built on. Your service depends on the CandidateRepository interface; Spring injects the implementation. That is why you can pass a mock in a unit test at all.

The test that matters more than the acronym
When you need to change something, how many files do you have to open, and how confident are you that nothing else broke?
If the answer is "many" and "not very", go back and ask which principle the code is fighting. That question is more useful than checking the five off a list.
4.6 Design patterns you will meet
You do not need to memorise a catalogue. You do need to recognise the patterns already running inside the framework you are using, because Spring is largely an assembly of them — and once you can name what Spring is doing, its behaviour stops being magic and starts being predictable.
The table below maps each pattern to the exact place you will run into it in this project. Learn them there, in context, rather than from a textbook example about shapes and animals.

Pattern
Where you meet it
Why it is there
Dependency Injection
Every Spring bean, Week 2 onward
Inverts control of construction so a class never builds its own collaborators — and so tests can supply different ones.
Singleton
The default Spring bean scope
One shared instance per context. Understand it, because it is also why a stateful field on a service is a bug waiting to happen.
Factory
BeanFactory, and your own token creation
Centralises construction when what gets built depends on runtime input.
Builder
Fluent APIs, DTOs, test data builders
Readable construction of objects with many optional fields, without a six-argument constructor.
Proxy
How @Transactional and @PreAuthorize actually work
Spring wraps your bean in a proxy to add behaviour around your method. This explains the self-invocation trap in Week 2 — a call to this.method() bypasses the proxy entirely.
Chain of Responsibility
The Spring Security filter chain (Week 3)
Each filter handles a request or passes it along. Once you see the chain as this pattern, Week 3 gets substantially easier.
Strategy
AuthenticationProvider, PasswordEncoder, CV parsers (Week 4)
Swappable algorithms behind one interface. This is what makes adding LDAP later a configuration change rather than a rewrite.
Template Method
JdbcTemplate, RestTemplate, abstract test bases
The framework owns the skeleton of the algorithm; you fill in the parts that vary.
Adapter
Wrapping PDFBox and POI behind your own interface (Week 4)
Keeps a third-party library at arm's length so replacing it later touches one class.
Specification
Dynamic candidate search (Week 4)
Composable query predicates. The reason your search does not become a wall of string concatenation.
Repository
Every Spring Data interface
A collection-like abstraction over persistence, so business logic never talks SQL.
Observer
Spring application events (bonus work)
Lets a state change publish an event without knowing or caring who reacts to it.
State
The application stage machine (bonus work)
Legal transitions live in the domain rather than in a growing if/else block in a controller.
DTO
Every API boundary, Week 2 onward
Separates what you store from what you expose, so a schema change is not automatically a breaking API change.

A warning about patterns
The most common mistake with patterns is reaching for one because you have just learned it. A pattern is a solution to a specific recurring problem; applied without that problem, it is just extra indirection someone else has to read through.
In a review you may be asked why you used a pattern. "It seemed like good practice" is a weak answer. "The parsing logic had to vary by file type and I did not want a growing if/else" is a strong one.
The reverse also gets flagged: a 300-line method with six nested conditionals is a pattern-shaped problem going unaddressed.

Database fundamentals: relational modelling, normalisation to third normal form, primary and foreign keys, indexes, transactions and ACID.
SQL: joins, aggregation, subqueries, and reading a query plan at a basic level.
Git: branching strategy, rebase vs. merge, conflict resolution, review etiquette, and recovering a repository you have broken.
GitHub: issues, pull requests, branch protection, and an introduction to Actions.
Maven: lifecycle phases, dependency scopes, the wrapper, reading a dependency tree.

Week 2 — Spring Boot Core and Data Persistence
Theme: A REST service is a set of layers with clear responsibilities — not a controller with everything in it.
By the end of this week you can:
Build a layered service: controller, service, repository, with DTOs at the boundary and entities never crossing it.
Design RESTful resources and pick correct verbs and status codes.
Validate input with Bean Validation and return a consistent, useful error body.
Handle exceptions globally with @RestControllerAdvice rather than try/catch in controllers.
Map JPA relationships, explain lazy vs. eager loading, and identify an N+1 problem from the SQL log.
Write unit and slice tests for the code you just wrote, in the same pull request.
Justify your layering in terms of single responsibility and dependency inversion.
Topics to cover
Dependency injection in depth: constructor injection as the default, bean scopes, the application context lifecycle, and why a circular dependency is a design signal rather than a config problem.
What @SpringBootApplication expands into, and how auto-configuration decides what to wire.
SOLID applied to layering: each layer has one responsibility, services depend on repository interfaces rather than implementations, and a fat controller is a single-responsibility violation you can see with the naked eye.
REST design: resource naming, idempotency, pagination, filtering, versioning, and the real difference between 400, 401, 403, 404, 409 and 422.
DTO mapping, and why exposing JPA entities from a controller causes production incidents.
Spring Data JPA: repositories, derived queries, @Query, projections, pagination and sorting.
Relationships: @OneToMany, @ManyToOne, @ManyToMany, cascade types, orphan removal.
The N+1 problem — turn on SQL logging, watch it happen, fix it with a fetch join and an @EntityGraph.
Flyway: baseline migration, versioned changes, and why nobody edits an applied migration.
Transactions: @Transactional, propagation, and the self-invocation trap — which is the proxy pattern from Section 4.6 showing through.
Creational patterns in practice: Factory and Builder, and where each earns its place in a DTO or a domain object with many optional fields.
Configuration: profiles, @ConfigurationProperties, environment variables, keeping secrets out of the repository.
Testing basics, starting now: JUnit 5 and Mockito on the service layer, @WebMvcTest with MockMvc on controllers.

Week 3 — Spring Security and JWT Authentication
Theme: Review point 1. Security is not a library you add at the end — it is a filter chain you understand.
By the end of this week you can:
Draw the Spring Security filter chain from memory and say what each major filter does.
Distinguish authentication from authorisation, and Principal from Authentication from SecurityContext.
Configure a SecurityFilterChain bean using the modern lambda DSL.
Hash and verify passwords correctly with BCrypt, and explain why hashing is not encryption.
Issue, sign, parse and validate a JWT, and defend every design choice in it.
Implement role-based access control at both route and method level, and choose correctly between them.
Describe any security control you have added in terms of the specific threat it stops.
Topics to cover
The filter chain, traced in the debugger. Put a breakpoint in the chain and step through one authenticated request end to end. This single exercise is worth more than any amount of reading.
The filter chain as the chain-of-responsibility pattern, and AuthenticationProvider as the strategy pattern. Naming what the framework is doing turns it from magic into something you can predict.
Core abstractions: AuthenticationManager, AuthenticationProvider, UserDetailsService, UserDetails, GrantedAuthority, SecurityContextHolder.
Password storage: BCrypt work factor, salting, DelegatingPasswordEncoder, and the migration path when an algorithm ages out.
A custom UserDetailsService backed by your users table.
JWT in depth: header, payload, signature; HS256 vs. RS256 and when asymmetric signing matters; claims design; expiry; and why a signed token is readable by anyone holding it.
Writing a JWT authentication filter and registering it at the correct position in the chain.
Refresh tokens: rotation, reuse detection, revocation, and what logout actually means in a stateless system.
RBAC design for ADMIN, HR and INTERVIEWER — and least privilege, when it would be easier to give HR everything.
Route-level rules with requestMatchers and hasRole; method-level with @PreAuthorize and SpEL. Where each belongs and why.
CORS and CSRF, and why a stateless JWT API treats CSRF differently from a session-cookie application.
Deliberate misconfiguration: expose an endpoint you should not, leak a stack trace, issue a 30-day token — then find and fix all three.
Audit logging for authentication events: who logged in, when, from where, and what failed.
OAuth2 concepts, as learning rather than implementation: resource owner, client, authorisation server and resource server; authorisation code with PKCE; client credentials; and how OIDC layers identity on top. You are implementing your own JWT issuance rather than delegating to an authorisation server, but you should be able to explain what you would change if identity moved to an external provider — and configuring Spring Security as an OAuth2 resource server is a worthwhile afternoon if the week allows.
Threat modelling your own API against the OWASP API Security Top 10.

Week 4 — Candidate Management Service
Theme: The messy realities: files, unstructured data, concurrency and search.

Topics to cover
File handling: MultipartFile, size and type constraints, storing the file while keeping only a reference in the database, and why the original filename is untrusted input.
Text extraction with Apache PDFBox and Apache POI. CV parsing is genuinely hard and every commercial product gets it partly wrong — expect imperfection and measure it rather than hiding it.
Parsing heuristics: regex for email, phone and years of experience, and skills matched against a controlled vocabulary.
Design the parser properly: a CvParser strategy interface with one implementation per format, selected at runtime. Adding a third format should mean adding a class, not editing an if/else. Wrap PDFBox and POI behind your own adapter interfaces so a library swap touches one file.
Concurrency and asynchronous processing: @Async with a bounded executor, thread pools, and why a singleton bean with mutable state is a race condition waiting to happen.
Job status tracking so the caller can poll bulk upload progress.
Dynamic queries with Specifications; composing predicates; building optional filters correctly. This is the specification pattern from Section 4.6.
Pagination and sorting at scale, and why OFFSET gets slow.
Tagging: many-to-many modelling, tag normalisation, searching by tag combination.
Data protection: CVs are personal data. Lawful basis, retention and right to erasure, and what each means concretely for your schema and your delete endpoint.

7. Capstone Project Specification
Build a backend platform supporting the recruitment lifecycle: job creation and publishing, candidate sourcing and tracking, and application status. The deliverable is a secure, tested, documented REST API in a GitHub repository. No frontend is required; Swagger UI is the interface.

A single application with clean internal service boundaries is expected and correct at this scale. Splitting it into separately deployable services is out of scope for five weeks — recognising when not to distribute is a more valuable thing to learn than building a distributed system badly.
7.1 User & Authentication Service (Required)
Functional requirements
Registration and login for HR, Interviewer and Admin roles.
Role-based access control enforced at both route and method level.
JWT access token generation and validation, with refresh token rotation.
User profile management and administrative user listing.
Password change, requiring the old password.
Audit log of authentication events.
Acceptance criteria
Passwords are stored only as BCrypt hashes. No endpoint, log line or error response ever returns one.
Access tokens expire in 15 minutes or less; refresh tokens rotate on use and old ones are invalidated.
An unauthenticated request to a protected endpoint returns 401; an authenticated request without the required role returns 403. The distinction is correct everywhere.
An INTERVIEWER cannot reach any HR-only or ADMIN-only endpoint, verified by automated test.
A tampered or expired token is rejected with a clear error that leaks nothing.
No secrets appear in the repository; all are externalised to environment variables.
Every endpoint is documented in OpenAPI with accurate request and response schemas.
7.2 Candidate Management Service (Required)
Functional requirements
Single and bulk CV upload, accepting PDF and DOCX.
CV parsing extracting at minimum name, email, phone, skills and years of experience.
Candidate profile CRUD, with the CV file retained and downloadable.
Free-text and structured search across name, skill, tag and status, with pagination and sorting.
Tagging: create, assign, remove, and search by tag combination.
Job creation, publishing and closing.
Candidate application to a job, with status tracking.
Candidate deletion that genuinely removes personal data.
Acceptance criteria
Bulk upload of 20 CVs completes without blocking the request thread, and job progress is queryable.
Parsing accuracy is measured over a documented test set of at least 20 real-format CVs and reported honestly, including the failures.
Uploads are validated for MIME type and size; a renamed executable is rejected.
Search returns correct results across every combination of filters and is paginated.
No search query is built by string concatenation.
Listing 1,000 candidates issues a bounded number of SQL queries — the N+1 problem is demonstrably absent, shown with SQL logging on.
Only HR and ADMIN can create or delete candidates; INTERVIEWER has read access only.
Deleting a candidate removes the stored CV file as well as the database row.

7.4 Bonus work
Attempt only once both required services meet every acceptance criterion above. Half-finished bonus work counts for less than none.

LDAP integration
Understanding directory-backed authentication is genuinely worth having — in most enterprises corporate identity lives in Active Directory and the application owns only authorisation.
Scope: stand up OpenLDAP in Docker or use embedded UnboundID with seeded users and groups; add an LDAP AuthenticationProvider alongside the existing database provider; map directory groups onto application authorities.
The reason this is achievable as an add-on rather than a rewrite is that AuthenticationProvider is a strategy interface — this is the open/closed principle paying off in a way you can point at.
Material: Spring Security reference, "LDAP Authentication" chapter; and the LDAP chapters of Spring: Spring Security on LinkedIn Learning.

Application Tracking Service
Move a candidate through stages — Applied → Screening → Interview → Offer → Hired, with Rejected and Withdrawn as terminal states — enforcing legal transitions in the domain rather than the controller. This is the state pattern.
Append-only audit log recording actor, timestamp, from-state and to-state. An audit trail you can UPDATE is not an audit trail.
Recruiter and interviewer assignment, with authorisation that depends on assignment rather than role alone — a natural use for @PreAuthorize with SpEL against the domain object.
Interview feedback with structured scoring across weighted criteria, and an aggregate result.
Spring application events to decouple side effects from the transition logic — the observer pattern, and the open/closed principle again.
7.5 Final presentation
Twenty minutes plus ten minutes of questions, to an audience that includes at least one non-engineer.
Problem and scope: what was built, and what was deliberately left out (3 min).
Architecture walkthrough with a diagram (4 min).
Live demo of the working system (7 min).
Security model: the controls, and the threats they address (3 min).
What went wrong, what was learned, what you would do differently (3 min).
Questions (10 min).

Be straight in the "what went wrong" section
That section matters, and it is usually where the strongest answers come from. "My first schema design forced me into a painful migration in Week 4, and here is what I would do differently" shows exactly the reflective capacity that predicts a good engineer.
Claiming everything went smoothly reads as either not paying attention or not being straight with the audience. This is said in advance so nobody plays it safe.
